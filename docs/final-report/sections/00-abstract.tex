\begin{abstract}
We designed and implemented a cooperative video storage system that
streams replicated segments to browser clients. Storage Peers verify
and serve segment data, while a Raft-replicated Controller service
publishes a video once it confirms 2 copies of every segment on distinct
Peers. The Controllers record repair tasks so that a new leader can
resume unfinished work. After a Peer fails, surviving holders share their
upload bandwidth between viewers and replica repair. We study how repair
timing and bandwidth limits affect playback and replica recovery.
In paired experiments on a single host, viewers download segments
over HTTP and consume them on a simulated media clock.

Under high demand, enabling shared repair increased mean post-fault stall
by 1.22\,s per viewer, while 7 of 18 sessions completed with or without
repair. Extending the repair grace period from 1 to 8\,s avoided copying
during 4\,s outages. During persistent outages, the same change increased
one-copy exposure by 16\%. A load-aware repair cap reduced stalls in one
failure context but showed no consistent advantage over a fixed 50\% cap
across arrival patterns. Once repair was verified, the remaining Peer
served all 9 segments in all 6 trials in which both original holders
stopped. These results show how repair can preserve access after a second
failure at a measurable cost to viewers sharing the surviving sources.
\end{abstract}
